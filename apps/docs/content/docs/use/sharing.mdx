Grant another user view or edit access to a subshell, and see exactly what each level exposes.

A subshell is **private to its owner by default**: it is absent from every other user's lists, detail pages, logs, and terminal views. It is not hidden behind a refusal; it is absent, answered as "not found", so ids cannot be probed for existence. Sharing is a deliberate widening you grant, and this page states what it opens.

## Granting and revoking

From the subshell's ⋯ menu, **Share…** lets you grant a level to **Everyone** (every signed-in user on the instance) or to named users:

- **view** lets a grantee see it: the list entry, the detail page, the stored transcript, and a live read-only terminal.
- **edit** adds driving it: typing into the live terminal, renaming it, restarting it, and terminating it. The terminate route honors an edit grant even though the interface stopped offering the button.

The grant list is replaced whole on every save, so revoking is removing the entry. An empty list means the subshell is private again.

Two parts of this model never change hands:

- **Owner-only, never conferred by any grant, and not held by admins either**: closing (deleting) the subshell, managing its shares, and its notification bell.
- **Admins hold instance-wide edit**: the ability to read and drive any subshell, deliberately without delete or re-share. An admin can rescue a stuck agent; they cannot destroy yours or widen it further.

Sharing is a human act performed in a browser, and the shares routes refuse machine credentials entirely. Elsewhere, a subshell's own token acts as its owner's key on the owner's own subshells only: a running agent never picks up its owner's shared grants, and it can never touch a foreign or shared one.

> [!important] What a share exposes
> A pane is not a curated document:
>
> - A **view** grant shows the grantee the subshell's full output, live. If a secret is on screen (a pasted token, a `.env` in a `cat`), it is shared.
> - An **edit** grant additionally exposes the keystroke stream and the ability to type into a live agent session.
> - **Everyone attached sees everyone else attached.** Each viewer announces its device name, the grid it displays, when it joined, whether its page is in the foreground, and whether it may type. That list goes to every viewer, including a view-only one. This is not an oversight: a pane has one size, set by its smallest visible viewer, and "why is my terminal 80 columns" has no answer without naming the other devices. Device names are sanitized on the way in, so no name carries control characters into your screen.
> - **Pushes do not follow the share.** Notifications stay targeted at the owner's devices: sharing widens who can watch, never who is notified.

## Disclosure indicators

Because these exposures are invisible from looking at a terminal, the interface states them. A shared subshell carries a permanent icon in its chrome; hovering names the reason. Widening an exposure raises a one-time banner. The banner is dismissible and can be turned off per device. The icon cannot be suppressed, because silencing an interruption is a preference while silencing the disclosure is not.

The same icon mechanism also flags a subshell running on a [node](/nodes) its viewer does not own: what anyone types there transits that machine.

> [!note] Node shares are a separate axis
> Sharing a subshell says who may watch or drive it. Making a machine launchable for other people is a different grant, with its own semantics: even a `view` node share lets the grantee launch their own private subshells there. That model lives on [Share a node](/nodes/sharing).
